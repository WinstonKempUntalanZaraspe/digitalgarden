## Material properties

Hardness refers to the ability of a material to resist abrasion, penetration, cutting action, or permanent distortion. Hardness may be increased by cold working the metal and, in the case of steel and certain aluminium alloys, by heat treatment. Hardness and strength are closely associated properties of metals. ==Brinell Test VS Rockwell Test VS Vickers Pyramid Test Hardness.==Strength: Ability of a material to resist deformation, resist stress without breaking. 

![ASML-1778056597628.webp\|241x305](/img/user/Atlas/Utilities/Images/ASML-1778056597628.webp) 

==Brittle:==A property that allows ==little==bending or deformation without shattering. Low energy absorption and extensive plastic deformation. Hard steel, cast aluminium and iron are brittle. (Undesirable property.)

==Ductile:== A property that permits the metal to be ==permanently== drawn, bent, twisted into shapes without breaking, essential for making wire and tubing. Preferred for aircraft as their ease of forming and resistance to failure under shock loads.

==Malleability:== A property that allows a metal that can be hammered, rolled, pressed into other shapes without breaking, or destroying the material. Necessary in sheet metal, worked into curved shapes like wingtips and fairings. Examples include==Gold and Copper==

==Elasticity:==A property that allows a metal to return to its original size and shape when force that makes it is removed. ==Remember the stress strain curve? Each metal has an elastic limit, exceeding it causes permanent distortion.==

# Steel

==Metal alloys==;When it is heated and temp. Increases, there are 2 critical temperatures.
- <mark style="background:#fff88f">Low critical temperature</mark> of metal alloy is the temperature at which the internal structure begins to change
- Then when temp is increased even further, and when the required change of internal structure is completed, the <mark style="background:#fff88f">upper critical temperature</mark> is achieved.

==Soaking:==The temperature of the furnace is held constant so rearrangement of internal structure of alloy can take place. Heavier parts require longer soaking to ensure equal heating throughout. 30 min to 1h.

==Cooling:==Related to the rate of cooling through critical range of metal alloy, determining form of internal structures which the alloy will retain.

==Hardening:==To harden, heat the steel to a temp. just above the upper critical point, soaking, or holding for a required length of time, then cooling rapidly by plunging the hot steel into oil/water/brine. Some metals can be cooled by leaving it in the air. Hardening by heat treatment will increase the hardness and strength, but be mindful, if properties are enhanced, other properties will be sacrificed, which in this case is ductility.
==Tempering:==Involves reheating the hardened steel alloy between the<span style='color: var(--mk-color-blue)'> lower</span> critical pt, holding it for a defined period to achieve hardness and strength, reducing brittleness by hardening and softening the steel.

==Annealing:==Heating the steel alloy just above the upper critical pt, soaking at that temp, and cooling very slowly in the furnace. This will produ[]()ce a coarse-grained soft, ductile metal without internal stresses or strains, increasing in toughness, ductility, and tensile strength is slightly improved/not affected.

==Bonus: Annealing + hardening:==
Ever wanted to shape a metal into a desired shape, but the alloy is not soft enough to work on? (forming, bending without breaking) You first need to anneal, to allow it to bend , then harden to increase hardness and strength!

==Normalizing:==Like annealing but the quenching process is rapider, causing a harder and stronger material, removing internal stresses by heat treating, welding, casting, forming, or machining. This only applies to iron base metals.

Plain carbon steels contain mainly Fe and C up to 2.0%, and contain Manganese, [Phosphorous, and Sulphur, both of which are impurities)]
==Nickel Steels==: Combine nickel with carbon steel. 3-3.75% nickels are commonly used. Nickle increases hardness, tensile strength, elastic limit of steel. Ductility is affected, but not appreciably.
==Chromium Steel:== High in hardness, strength, and corrosion resistant properties. The anti-corrosive degree of the steel is determined by surface condition of the metal, composition, temperature, and concentration. In aircraft, 18-8 is used. 18% chromium and 8% nickel.

==Principal alloy element: primary chemical elements added to a base metal (like iron, aluminum, or copper) to change its physical and mechanical properties. Pure metals are often too soft or reactive for industrial use. By adding other elements, engineers can create "recipes" that make the metal stronger, lighter, or more resistant to rust.==

### How it Works (The Science)

When you add a principal alloying element, it works on a microscopic level through two main methods:

1. **Substitutional Alloying:** The atoms of the alloying element are roughly the same size as the base metal atoms and simply take their place in the crystal lattice.
    
2. **Interstitial Alloying:** The alloying atoms are much smaller (like Carbon in Iron) and wedge themselves into the gaps between the larger metal atoms.
    

In both cases, these "foreign" atoms create **lattice distortion**. This makes it harder for the layers of atoms to slide past each other, which is exactly what makes the metal feel "harder" or "stronger" to us.

### Why "Principal"?

The term "principal" distinguishes these from **trace elements** or **impurities**. While a trace element might be present in less than 0.01%, a principal alloying element is added intentionally in specific, larger percentages to define the alloy's core identity (e.g., the 18% chromium that makes "18/8" stainless steel).

This is a "trick" question about the difficulty of the repair process. While scarf repairs are structurally superior, they are actually much **harder** to perform than stepped or patch repairs.

### Why the other options ARE reasons to use Scarf Repair:

- **Flush Finish:** Because you sand the parent material at a very shallow angle (a "scarf"), the repair plies sit perfectly level with the original surface. This is critical for aerodynamics (reducing drag) and aesthetics.
    
- **Ply Orientation:** When you sand a "scarf" (a slope), you reveal the different layers of the composite like the rings of a tree. This makes it very easy for a technician to see the fiber direction of each original ply so they can match them exactly with the new repair plies.
    
- **Contours:** Because a scarf is a smooth, continuous taper, it can "follow" the curves and complex shapes of an aircraft (like a wing's leading edge) much more naturally than rigid steps can.

### The Process: Wet Lay-Up

In a **wet lay-up**, you start with dry reinforcement fabric (like the black carbon fiber sheet in the photo) and manually add liquid resin.

- **The Action:** The technician "pours resin in the middle of the fabric" (as stated in the figure caption) and will then use a squeegee or roller to spread that resin until it completely soaks through the fibers.
    
- **"Impregnating":** This is the technical term for saturating the dry fibers with the liquid resin matrix.
    

---

### Why it isn't the other options:

- **Not for Pre-pregs:** Pre-pregs are manufactured in a factory. They arrive at the repair station already "pre-impregnated" with resin and backed with plastic film. You would never pour liquid resin onto a pre-preg in the shop; you would simply peel the backing off and stick it down.
    
- **Not "Lay-out":** In composite terminology, "lay-out" usually refers to the step of cutting the fabric to the correct size and shape or placing the plys into a mold in a specific orientation ($0^{\circ}$, $45^{\circ}$, $90^{\circ}$). The photo shows the **saturation** phase, which is specifically "impregnating."
    

### Summary for your Quiz

When you see a bottle of resin or a puddle of liquid being applied to a dry piece of black fabric, it is almost always a **Wet Lay-Up Impregnation** step. This is common for smaller, non-structural repairs or in shops that don't have the industrial freezers required to store pre-preg materials.

**Short answer: ALWAYS assume positive axes for pins. NEVER for rollers on slopes.**

For **A_x and A_y** at a pin, just assume both point in the positive direction (right and up). They are **completely independent** — the geometry tells you nothing about their relationship.

### 1. The Calculus Definition of Moment of Inertia

In physics, the true definition of the area moment of inertia about a central axis (let's use the X-axis as an example) is calculated using an integral:

$$I_x = \int y^2 \, dA$$

This formula states: "Take every tiny microscopic square piece of area ($dA$) in the cross-section, measure its perpendicular distance ($y$) from the bending axis, square that distance ($y^2$), and add them all up."

### 2. Setting up the Integral

Let’s look at a rectangle where we define the horizontal width as $b$ and the vertical thickness as $h$. The bending axis is the horizontal X-axis running through the exact center.

* A tiny strip of area ($dA$) running horizontally has a width of $b$ and an incredibly tiny thickness of $dy$. So, $dA = b \cdot dy$.
* The distance from the center axis to the very top edge of the rectangle is $\frac{h}{2}$, and to the bottom edge is $-\frac{h}{2}$.

Now, let's plug $dA = b \cdot dy$ into our calculus integral and integrate from the bottom edge to the top edge:

$$I_x = \int_{-\frac{h}{2}}^{\frac{h}{2}} y^2 \cdot (b \cdot dy)$$

### 3. Solving the Integral (Where the Cube Happens)

Because $b$ is just a constant width, we can pull it outside of the integral:

$$I_x = b \int_{-\frac{h}{2}}^{\frac{h}{2}} y^2 \, dy$$

When you take the integral of $y^2$, standard calculus rules state that it becomes $\frac{y^3}{3}$:

$$I_x = b \left[ \frac{y^3}{3} \right]_{-\frac{h}{2}}^{\frac{h}{2}}$$

Now, we substitute our upper limit ($\frac{h}{2}$) and lower limit ($-\frac{h}{2}$) into $y$:

$$I_x = b \left( \frac{(\frac{h}{2})^3}{3} - \frac{(-\frac{h}{2})^3}{3} \right)$$

$$I_x = b \left( \frac{\frac{h^3}{8}}{3} - \frac{-\frac{h^3}{8}}{3} \right)$$

$$I_x = b \left( \frac{h^3}{24} + \frac{h^3}{24} \right)$$

$$I_x = b \left( \frac{2h^3}{24} \right) = \frac{1}{12}bh^3$$

### Why you can't vice-versa the definition:

Look closely at how that math unfolded:

1. The dimension running **parallel** to the axis ($b$) stayed completely outside of the calculus power change. It just acted as a constant multiplier.
2. The dimension running **perpendicular** to the axis ($h$) was the one that provided the boundary limits ($y$) for the integration. Because we had to integrate $y^2 \, dy$, it mathematically forced that specific dimension to become cubed ($h^3$).

So, you cannot swap them because **the calculus physically dictates that whichever dimension represents the distance away from the bending axis *must* be the one that gets cubed.** By convention, engineers designated the letter "$h$" (height/depth) to represent that perpendicular distance, and "$b$" (base/width) to represent the parallel dimension. If you choose to flip the definition, you would have to change your formula to $I = \frac{1}{12}hb^3$, which means you are still cubing the exact same physical dimension anyway!

That tension-free, compression-free slice is the **Neutral Axis**, and for symmetrical shapes like rectangles, it always passes perfectly through the **geometric center (centroid)**.
